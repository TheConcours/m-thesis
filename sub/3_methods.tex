\section{Proposed Waveform Design Techniques}
\label{sec:methods}
% This section investigates methodologies for the mitigation of leakage errors in $X$ and $CZ$ gates, beginning with
% the $X$ gate. Our theoretical framework builds upon the Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate (DRAG)
% formalism. Conventionally, the DRAG-1 scheme utilizes a predefined seed function to synthesize a control pulse that

In this section, we propose methods to mitigate leakage errors in $X$ and $CZ$ gates, beginning with the $X$ gate. Our
approach builds upon the recursive DRAG schemes which conventionally rely on seed functions to generate a control pulse to
minimize leakage caused by two photon transitions. However, this conventional method suffers from limited control over
the resulting pulse shape. To address this, we establish a novel method to construct DRAG-1 pulses without relying on
a seed function, thereby enabling shorter gate durations without compromising operational fidelity.

\subsection{Inverse Design of the DRAG-1 Pulse}
% Although in \ref{subsec:drag}, the DRAG-1 pulse is created from the seed pulse waveform $\Omega_1$
% through \eref{eq:drag1}, now we reverse this process.
% First we solve \eref{eq:drag1} for $\Omega_1(t)$.
We now rewrite  \eref{eq:drag1} as a second-order linear oridnary differential equation (ODE):
\begin{equation}
  \Omega^2_x(t)=\left(1+\frac{D^2}{\Delta_2^2}\right)\Omega_1^2(t).
  \label{eq:drag1_ode}
\end{equation}
Recognizing this as a driven harmonic oscillator equation, the general solution is
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_1^2(t) = \int_0^t\,\left[\dfrac{1}{\Delta_2}\sin(\Delta_2(t-\tau))\right][\Delta_2^2\Omega_x(\tau)^2]\,\dd \tau
  + A\cos(\Delta_2t+\phi).
  \label{eq:drag1_sol}
\end{equation}
The first term represents the particular solution to the ODE in \eref{eq:drag1_ode}.
To check the validity of this particular solution we use the Leibniz rule \cite{apostol}, which dictates the differentiation under the integral sign:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dt} \int_{a(t)}^{b(t)} F(x,t) dx = F(b(t), t) \cdot b'(t) - F(a(t), t) \cdot a'(t) + \int_{a(t)}^{b(t)} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} F(x,t) dx.
  \label{eq:leibniz}
\end{equation}
% According to this formula, we obtain the first time derivative
% \begin{align*}
%   \dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}\Omega_1(t)^2 & = \int_0^t\,\left[\cos(\Delta_2(t-\tau))\right][\Delta_2^2\Omega_x(\tau)^2]\,\dd \tau -\Delta A\sin(\Delta_2 t+\phi)
%   % \dfrac{\dd^2}{\dd t^2}\Omega_1(t)^2 & =\int_0^t\,\left[\cos(\Delta_2(t-\tau))\right][\Delta_2^2\Omega_x(\tau)^2]\,\dd \tau
% \end{align*}
Recall we have three boundary conditions on our debt:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\Omega_x = 0$ at both ends ($t=0$, $T$): This ensures compatibility with the SWT and enforces boundary continuity for the X-pulse, mitigating spectral leakage.
  \item $\dot{\Omega}_x = 0$ at both ends ($t=0$, $T$): This condition guarantees a smooth envelope derivative, maintaining essential boundary continuity for the corresponding Y-pulse.
  \item $\Omega_1 = 0$ at both ends ($t=0$, $T$): This further enforces strict SWT compatibility at the start and completion of the gate.
\end{enumerate}

Applying the third boundary condition at the initial time $t = 0$ immediately eliminates the homogeneous component, yielding $A = 0$ in \eref{eq:drag1_sol}.
We name
$$
  \Omega_1^2(t) = \int_0^t\,\left[\dfrac{1}{\Delta_2}\sin(\Delta_2(T-\tau))\right][\Delta_2^2\Omega_x(\tau)^2]\,\dd \tau
$$
the DRAG-1 formula in integral form. In the same parlance, we call \eref{eq:drag1} the differential DRAG-1 formula.
Imposing the boundary constraint at $t=T$ yields
$$
  \left(\Omega_1^2(T)\right) = 0 = \int_0^T\,\left[\dfrac{1}{\Delta_2}\sin(\Delta_2(T-\tau))\right][\Delta_2^2\Omega_x(\tau)^2]\,\dd \tau.
$$
Differentiating both sides with respect to $t$, we obtain
$$
  \left(\Omega_1(T)\dot{\Omega}_1(T)\right) = 0 = \int_0^T\,\left[\cos(\Delta_2(T-\tau))\right][\Delta_2^2\Omega_x(\tau)^2]\,\dd \tau.
$$
These two constraints combined implies
$$
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[\Delta_2^2\Omega_x(\tau)^2]e^{-i\Delta\tau}\,\dd \tau = 0.
$$
Consequently, the boundary condition at $t=T$  translates to a null fourier transform condition
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^T \Omega_x(\tau)^2 e^{-i\Delta_2\tau} d\tau = 0.
  \label{eq:drag1_fourier}
\end{equation}
\subsection{Inverse Design of the DRAG-2 Pulse}
Recall that the DRAG-2 pulse in \eref{eq:drag2} has exactly the same form as the DRAG-1 in \eref{eq:drag1}.
Since the boundary conditions coming from the SWTs also have the same form ($ \Omega_2(t) = 0 \text{ at } t=0,\,T. $),
we find that the existence of such a $\Omega_2$ in \eref{eq:drag2} is guaranteed by
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^T \Omega_1(\tau)^2 e^{-i\Delta_3\tau} d\tau = 0,
  \label{eq:drag2_fourier}
\end{equation}
which is equivocal to $\mathcal{F}[\Omega_1](\Delta_3)=0$.
By taking Fourier transform of the both sides of the differential DRAG-1 formula \eref{eq:drag1},
we obtain
$$
  \mathcal{F}[\Omega_x^2](z) = (1-\dfrac{z^2}{\Delta_2^2})\mathcal{F}[\Omega_1^2](z).
$$
Hence, \eref{eq:drag2_fourier} is equivalent to $\mathcal{F}[\Omega_x](\Delta_3)=0$.

\subsubsection{DRAG-1 pulse}
\label{subsubsec:drag1_method}
We propose a novel method to synthesize a flat-top DRAG-1 pulse. Utilizing a flat-top envelope enables us to reduce the
lower bound of the gate duration for which the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation (SWT) remains valid. This is because
keeping the peak amplitude, $\max\vert{}\Omega_x\vert{}$, relatively small helps mitigate the inaccuracies introduced by
the early truncation of the SWT series.To engineer a pulse that yields a zero Fourier transform at a specific target
frequency, the echo method is a widely employed technique. This method is ubiquitous across numerous scientific and
technological disciplines, including signal processing, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) \cite{Jenkins}, acoustics
\cite{Pal2018}, and laser physics \cite{Ma2021}. Its direct application to quantum gate operations has been previously
explored in Ref. \cite{Polat2026}.
Although Jesus et al. \cite{99999} investigated optimal pulse waveforms by designing
$\Omega_x$ indirectly via the auxiliary function $\Omega_1$, our methodology allows for the direct design of a DRAG-1
pulse using \eref{eq:drag1_fourier}.



Let $t_d=\pi/\Delta$. For a given function $f(t)$, its delayed superposition $f(t)+f(t-t_d)$'s Fourier transform evaluated at $\Delta$ is
$$
  \mathcal{F}[f(t)+f(t-t_d)](\Delta) = \mathcal{F}[f](\Delta)+e^{i\Delta t_d}\mathcal{F}[f](\Delta) = 0.
$$
We aim to design a pulse such that this sum results in a flat-top profile, as illustrated in \fref{fig:drag1}.
Here we also impose that $f(t)$ is a flat-top pulse, then the pulse design problem reduces to defining the waveform
strictly on $[0,t_r]$, where $t_r$ denotes the rise time.

By normalizing this rising edge, the objective can be restated as finding a function $g(t)$ on $[-1,1]$ that satisfies
the following property:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \label{itm:g1} $g$ is anti-symmetric.
  \item \label{itm:g2} $g$ is monotonically increasing.
  \item \label{itm:g3} $g(1)=1/2$.
  \item  \label{itm:g4} The first and second derivative of $g$vanish at $t=-1$.
\end{enumerate}
The first condtion comes from the additional constraint that the $f(t)$ is also a flat-top pulse.
The second condtion is set so that it maximally reduces gate duration.
The fourth condition ensures that $\Omega_x=\dot{\Omega}=0$ are satisfied at $t=0,\,T$.
% $g$ represents the function on $[0,t_r]$.
Once obtaining such a $g$, we reconstruct $\Omega_x^2$ as
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_x^2(t) =
  \begin{cases}
    A \left(g(2t/t_r-1)-g(-1)\right) \quad                     & (0<t<t_r)       \\
    A                         \quad                            & (t_r<t<t_d)     \\
    A\left( g(2(t_r+t_d-t)/t_r-1)-g(-1)\right)           \quad & (t_d<t<t_d+t_r) \\
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:drag1_flattop}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the peak amplitude, determined by integrating $\Omega_x^2$ according to \eref{eq:xgate_condition}.

\subsubsection{DRAG-2 pulse}
We now extend the method described above and gain a pulse which also satisfies the DRAG-2 formula.
Note that for a function $f(t)$,
$$iz\mathcal{F}[f](z) = \mathcal{F}[f^\prime](z).$$
This implies that the requirement that $\Omega_x^2$ have a zero Fourier transform at $\Delta_3$
is equivalent to its derivate have a zero Fourier transform at $\Delta_3$.
If $h(t)$ is the derivative of $g(t)$, $h(t)=g^\prime(t)$, the derivative of the flat-top pulse in \ref{subsubsec:drag1_method} consists of two
$h(t)$ and $-h(t-T+t_r)$ as depicted in \fref{fig:drag2}.
$h(t)$ is a symmetric function since it is a derivative of a anti-symmetric function.

We can create $h(t)$ so that it has zero Fourier transform at $\Delta_3$ using the echo method as illustrated in
\fref{fig:drag2}.
Theoretically, $\Delta_n = \dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\Delta$, so the delay time to cancel the Fourier transform is

the Riccati equation becomes
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    i\dot{x}_1 & = (2\delta+\Delta) x_1 - \frac{1}{2}(\Omega_x + i\Omega_y)x_2 - \frac{\lambda}{2}(\Omega_x - i\Omega_y)x_1 x_2,                                         \\
    i\dot{x}_2 & = \frac{\lambda}{2}(\Omega_x + i\Omega_y) + (2\delta+\Delta) x_2 - \frac{1}{2}(\Omega_x - i\Omega_y)x_1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}(\Omega_x - i\Omega_y)x_2^2.

\begin{equation}

    A & = -\frac{1}{2}(x_2^2+\lambda x_1), \\

\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\Omega} = \Omega_x+i\Omega_y = \dfrac{A^\star C - B C^\star}{|A|^2-|B|^2}, \label{eq:riccati_Omega}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \delta(t) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{i\dot{x}_1 + \frac{1}{2} x_2 \tilde{\Omega} + \frac{1}{2}\lambda x_1 x_2 \tilde{\Omega}^*}{x_1} - \Delta_2 \right) \label{eq:riccati_delta}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Variational Pulse Adjustment}
% \cite{Werschnik_2007}
A direct appraoch to applying \eref{eq:riccati_Omega} and \eref{eq:riccati_delta} involves
finding $x_1$ and $x_2$ that lands on $0$ at $t=0,\,T$, and the time evolution given by \eref{eq:riccati_timeevolution}
corresponds to a $X$ gate.
At the very end of this process, we find a numerical solution for $\tilde{\Omega}$ and $\delta$.
However, this methodology introduces several analytical and numerical challenges.
Most notably, the non-linearity of the Riccati equation \eref{eq:riccati} substantially complicates
the precise calibration of gate operations.
To circumvent this issue, we reframe the pulse shaping problem within a continuous optimization framework utilizing a
variational method.
From existing $\tilde{\Omega}$ and $\delta$, we numerically solve the corresponding $x_1$ and $x_2$,
then we add variations to thus obtained $x_1$ and $x_2$ to reduce leakage errors and achieve higher fidelity operations.
To preserve the X gate condition \eref{eq:xgate_condition},
\begin{equation}
  \delta \Theta = \int_0^T \sum_{k=1}^2 \left( \frac{\partial \Omega_x}{\partial x_k} \delta x_k + \frac{\partial \Omega_x}{\partial \dot{x}_k} \delta \dot{x}_k + \text{c.c.} \right) dt
\end{equation}
must vanish. ($\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_i}$ denotes Wirtinger derivatives.)
Integral by parts transforms the above equation into
\begin{equation}
  \delta \Theta = \left[ \sum_{k=1}^2 \left( P_k(T) \delta x_k(T) + P_k^*(T) \delta x_k^*(T) \right) \right] + \int_0^T \sum_{k=1}^2 \left( G_k(t) \delta x_k(t) + G_k^*(t) \delta x_k^*(t) \right) dt,
  \label{eq:riccati_variation}
\end{equation}
where $G_k(t)$ and $P_k(t)$ ($k=1,2$) are defined as
\begin{align*}
  G_k(t) & = \frac{\partial \Omega_x}{\partial x_k} - \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial \Omega_x}{\partial \dot{x}_k} \right), \\
  P_1(t) & = \frac{\partial \Omega_x}{\partial \dot{x}_1} = i \frac{A^* - B^*}{2(\vert{}A\vert{}^2 - \vert{}B\vert{}^2)} x_2,   \\
  P_2(t) & = \frac{\partial \Omega_x}{\partial \dot{x}_2} = -i \frac{A^* - B^*}{2(\vert{}A\vert{}^2 - \vert{}B\vert{}^2)} x_1.
\end{align*}

Note that we have $\delta x_k(0) = 0$ since we already have $x_k(0)=0$ as an initial value of the integration.

To mitigate the coupling of computational and non-computational subspaces,
we want $x_1(T)$ and $x_2(T)$ as small as possible.

Ideally, we can totally cancel the leakage error by setting $\delta x_k (T) = -x_k(T)$.
However, since this is a variational method, $\delta x_k(t)$ should be sufficiently small and
in practice, we multiply some small number $\epsilon$ to thus obtained $x_k(t)$.

\subsection{$CZ$ gate}
In this section, for brevity, the length of pulses are assumed to be $N$, an odd number.
As seen in \ref{subsec:leakage},
if we denote with $g$ the derivative of $\theta$ we design in non-linear time frame,
the leakage error is approximately expressed as $\dfrac{1}{4}|\mathcal{F}[g](\Delta)|^2$.
Although both formulations ($X$ gate and $CZ$ gate) share the similar objective of minimizing or zeroing out the Fourier transform, the specific
methods we employ for each gate differ. This distinction arises because, in practical applications of the $CZ$ gate, it is
more critical to maintain a small Fourier transform amplitude over an interval of frequencies \cite{Martinis2014}.

In the following, we propose three methods to achieve this.
One is a proposal of a novel pulse shape.
The other two are  optimization techniques with sequential quadratic programming.
\subsubsection{The Anti-symmetric Chebyshev Pulse}
Maintaining a specified sidelobe level, Chebyshev pulse is the signal that minimizes the mainlobe width.

Mathematically, a chebyshev pulse $g_{ch}$ is a discrete time pulse defined as
$$
  g_{\text{ch}}[n] = \frac{1}{N} \Bigg[ 1 + 2r \sum_{k=0}^{(N-1)/2} (-1)^k T_{N-1}\left(x_0 \cos \frac{\pi k}{N}\right)  \cos \left( \frac{2\pi k}{L} \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) \right) \Bigg], $$
where $r = \frac{1}{T_{N-1}(x_0)}$ corresponds to the suppressed sidelobe level.
We call its anti-symmetrization
$$
  g_{ach}[n] = \begin{cases}
    g_{ch}[n]  \quad     & n< (N+1)/2  \\
    0             \quad  & n = (N+1)/2 \\
    -g_{ch}[N-n]   \quad & n > (N+1)/2 \\
  \end{cases},
$$
anti-symmetric Chebyshev pulse $g_{ach}$.

\subsubsection{Spectrum Suppression via Sequential Quadratic Programming}


There are many methods known to tailor a pulse with a target spectrum.
Weighted Chebyshev approximation (WCA) \cite{Ding2025} is one of them.
In WCA, you have two prescribed parameters, a weight function $W(w)$ and a spectrum profile
$A(w)$. WCA is a method to find a pulse whose spectrum is $G(w)$ such that the maximum $L^\infty$ error
$$
  \max\{|W(w)(G(w)-A(w))|\}
$$
is minimized.
WCA has the following disadvantages.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{over-constraining}
        Although the  goal is to optimize the ``amplitude'' (intensity) of the spectrum while the phase is left
        arbitrary, because the WCA objective function $\max\{\vert{}W(w)(G(w) - A(w))\vert{}\}$ is
        evaluated in the complex domain, we need to design ``good'' choice of phase beforehand, making the implementation
        of algorithm difficult.

  \item\textbf{Outliers}
        By the very nature of Chebyshev approximation, WCA seeks to minimize the ``maximum error'' across the entire
        evaluation function (minimizing the $\mathcal{L}_\infty$ norm). This means that if the spectrum deviates
        significantly from the target at even a single point (such as a sharp peak or a minor dip), the algorithm will
        focus excessively on correcting that specific outlier, risking an overall distorted pulse waveform.
\end{itemize}

In the following, we present two formulations of our optimization problem.
We formulate them in a form solvable in the framework of Sequential Quadratic Programming \cite{powell1978fast}.

\subsubsection{Redefining the Slepian eigenvalue problem}
Recall from \ref{subsec:slepian_pulses},
% $\dfrac{1}{4}|\mathcal{F}[g](\Delta)|^2$.
the (second) Slepian pulse is the solution of the second largest eigenvalue to the following problem
\begin{equation}
  \dfrac{\int_{-W}^{W}\,|G_{\text{DTFT}}(\omega)|^2 d\omega}
  {\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\,|G_{\text{DTFT}}(\omega)|^2 d\omega} = \dfrac{g^TA_{m,n}g}{g^Tg} \label{eq:slepproblem}
\end{equation}
where $A_{m,n} = 2\sinc((m-n)W) = \dfrac{\sin(2\pi(m-n)W)}{\pi(m-n)W}$, the Fourier transform of a rectangular function.
(\ref{eq:slepproblem}) is the eigenvalue problem of the matrix $A_{m,n}$. It was Slepian who found a commuting
matrix for $A_{m,n}$ and solve the problem analyitcally.

% Close investigation of the \eref{eq:leakageerror}
Suppose our target range of the gate duration satisfies For the target range of $W_1\leq \dfrac{T}{N}  \Delta \leq W_2$.
Then the original maximization problem turns into the following minimization problem:
$$\text{minimize } \frac{\int_{W_1}^{W_2} \vert{}G_{\text{DTFT}}(\omega)\vert{}^2 d\omega}{\int_{W_1}^{W_2} \vert{}G_{\text{DTFT}}(\omega)\vert{}^2 d\omega} = \frac{\tilde{g}^T (A_{\text{rect}}(W_2) - A_{\text{rect}}(W_1)) \tilde{g}}{\tilde{g}[0]^2 + \dots + \tilde{g}[N]^2}$$
The conventional use of Slepian pulse is quite casual about the target gate time range of operation, and Slepian pulse
is the best pulse if the above $W_2$ is set as $W_2=\pi$.

The difficulty arising by reformulating the problem in this fashion is the problem is no longer anhalytically solvable.
To clarify the search space of $g$, we impose the following conditions:
To not elongate gate time we impose the following condition,
% $\tilde{g}$ should be anti-symmetric and non-negative on the first half.
\begin{itemize}
  \item ${g}[s] = {g}[N-1-s]$: (anti-symmetricity) To mitigate $1/f$ noise.
  \item ${g}[s] \geq 0 \quad \text{for }0\leq s\leq L-1$ : (positivity) To not elongate gate duration.
\end{itemize}
If we define,
$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\  &  & 1\\0 & \cdots & 0 \\  &  & -1 \\& \mathinner{\mkern2mu\raise1pt\hbox{.}\mkern2mu\raise4pt\hbox{.}\mkern2mu\raise7pt\hbox{.}\mkern1mu} & \\ -1 & &  \end{pmatrix}, \quad C = B^T A B,$$
$$\begin{pmatrix} \tilde{g}[0] \\ \vdots \\ \tilde{g}[N] \end{pmatrix} = B \begin{pmatrix} h[0] \\ \vdots \\ h[L-1] \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then
$$\text{maximize } -\frac{h^T C h}{h^T h},$$
with $h$ being a positive vector of length $\lfloor N\rfloor/2$ is the problem to solve.

\subsubsection{Sidelobe-Aware Optimization}
Unlike the formulation given above, the second formulation is more of a heuristic sort, more focused on the initial pulse
